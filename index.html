01 Lenskart@Home ● Live
₹1.8Cr
Personalised Home Trials
The Problem

Four in five Home Trial visitors left at step one, before picking an address or slot. So we called the ones who had shown intent and asked why.

“The store is already close to my house. If I go there, I'll get much more variety.”

Recurring verbatim from multiple discovery calls
Customer Insight

Customers weren't rejecting home trials. They were rejecting a kit they had no say over.

No say over the frames made booking feel like a gamble. A store visit felt certain. This was a control problem, not a distance problem.

30%
of returns on online orders were for reasons a home trial would catch: too heavy, doesn't suit me, wrong colour, uncomfortable. Source: my analysis of online return reasons.
Constraint

No real backend or inventory investment until demand was proven. Test cheaply first.

Decision

Test control in isolation. A 3-week, pan-India pilot, measured on completed bookings against the weekly baseline.

The MVP: Testing Control, Not Variety

Our bet: customers didn't need more frames. They needed the act of choosing. We let them pick frames on an existing product listing page, from the same ~150 frames the agent already carried. Nothing changed in the backend. The agent brought the same kit, now hand-picked by the customer.

If bookings moved without one new frame, choice was the lever, not variety. It was the cheapest test we could run.

+7%
Completed Home Trial bookings in the 3-week pilot vs the weekly baseline. The hypothesis held.
The Real Launch: Real Inventory, Real Choice

Then we built it properly. Customers browse real inventory at their nearby store and choose the exact frames to bring home. Live in 5 cities.

Bonus: the same store inventory now serves walk-ins and home trials. The revenue itself comes from the lift in bookings.

“This is a great feature, we were too busy to go to the store so decided to do a home trial; you should make it a paid service, it is so premium.”

Customer who ended up purchasing 3 frames
Evidence

Nobody asked them to. Once agents could see customers' picks, they started calling ahead to talk frames. We built a selection feature. Agents turned it into a styling concierge.

The Bigger Problem

Launching home trials was the easy half. Scaling meant turning every Lenskart store into a home trial hub.

  • Gives stores a reason to reach beyond walk-in customers
  • Gives online customers a real, personal store connect
  • Adds home trials to what a store can offer, not just what an agent delivers

That raised a harder question first: which store owns which customer?

Wanna see how we solved that?

02 Lenskart · Truly Omni Channel · ● Live
19-point NPS increase
Online-to-Offline (O2O) JourneyTruly Omnichannel Experience for Customers
The Problem

In-store, a staff member can nudge a customer toward a repair or exchange when the fit feels off. Online customers get no one. Something feels wrong, so they return it.

7%
Return rate for offline (in-store) orders
18%
Return rate for online orders

The fix: make every store accountable for a territory. Every customer in it, online, offline or home trial, is that store's responsibility.

Three Approaches Evaluated

All three tried to infer which store a customer has a relationship with. Wrong question. None went live: each was rejected on analysis.

Distance-based

Nearest store within a fixed 10km radius.

✗ Rejected  ·  Overlaps and dead zones left no single store to own targets
Last QMS (Eye Test) Store

The store where the customer last had an eye test.

✗ Rejected  ·  Only 31% of QMS stores matched the customer's actual nearest store
Combination

QMS where available, distance everywhere else.

✗ Rejected  ·  Two attribution classes, impossible to explain or set fair targets against
Polygon Attribution

One accountable store per address, built from 0.1km² tiles and a 30-minute drive time.

✓ Chosen  ·  No overlap, no dead zones, auditable ownership, explainable targets. Cost: it picks the store responsible for a customer, not the one they prefer.
Evidence

Why “last visited” broke down. Customers who bought far from home had shopped near their workplace, wanted more variety, or didn't know a closer store existed. Last visit wasn't loyalty. It was coincidence.

The goal was never to find the store a customer prefers. It's to find the store best positioned to physically serve them.

The Solution: Polygon Attribution

Polygon attribution swaps the radius rule for drive-time territories. From Walmart's hex-grid catchment model we took one idea: build territories from small tiles (0.1km²). Instead of sizing by store capacity, each territory follows a 30-minute drive. Territories change only when the store network does, so ownership stays auditable and targets explainable.

Decision

Trade-off we accepted: accountability over preference. Polygons decide who owns an order, not where the customer would shop. 25% of customers bought from stores 5km+ away, on the way to work or mid-errand. We haven't measured how often the assigned store isn't the one they'd pick. We chose accountability anyway: without one owner per address, there are no fair targets and no one responsible for the 48-hour check-in.

The Dashboard: One Book of Business

Every store got a “Your Territory” view in its POS: its polygon, online and walk-in orders together, one set of targets. Plus two tools to act on it:

  • 48-hour check-in calls: stores call online customers after delivery to catch issues early and steer a repair or exchange instead of a return.
  • Proactive home trial pitches: stores reach online customers in their territory who dropped off mid-browse. “We have this frame in store and can bring it to your home.”

Live store POS; territory map + unified revenue view

41 → 60
Online customer NPS in one month where the assigned store did the 48-hour check-in. The no-call control cohort stayed low. Return-rate impact is still being measured.
03 Bajaj Finserv Health · Health Tech · Data ● Live
8× Health Files adoption
Smart Health Reports: Rebuilding a Dormant Feature
The Dead Feature

Health Files let customers upload lab reports. Almost nobody did. Instead of polishing the upload flow, we asked customers why.

Evidence

The few who used it uploaded everything from prescriptions to Aadhaar cards. Health Files had become a document locker nobody asked for.

“Why give my reports to a platform that does nothing with them?”

What customers actually told us
The Technical Challenge

Every lab names, formats and measures the same parameter differently. We had to standardise before we could explain anything.

📄 Raw PDF
Lab reports in different formats, layouts, units
🔍 OCR
Extract every parameter, value, and unit from the document
🔗 LOINC Mapping
Map each parameter to its universal medical ID; normalise across labs
🌟 Smart Report
Plain English explanations + historical trend for every parameter
Decision

Scope by Pareto. The top 20% of AHC packages cover 80% of parameters, so we built those packages first and deferred the long tail.

The Product

Each parameter in plain English: what it means, where you stand, what's critical and how to improve, plus a trend across every past report.

Outcome
  • Active Health Files users grew 8× within three months of launch.
  • A dormant upload flow became a report people came back to.
  • ₹7L incremental MRR from health-service recommendations surfaced inside the report.
Evidence

75% of customers googled out-of-range values the moment a report arrived. We replaced that anxious searching with plain-language context and an optional route to care.

04 Bajaj Finserv Health · ● Live
+18% CTR
Doctor Quality Score & Listing Reorder
The Problem

Doctor listings had no real ranking. Doctors who missed appointments ranked level with reliable ones, and profiles missing a degree, experience or even a photo could show up in the first few scrolls.

What Patients Actually Weigh

We identified the signals patients use to choose a doctor and set their weights from behavioural data and patient interviews. The weights were a hypothesis, not a permanent truth. The live result is in the outcome below.

Signals 1–3 feed the Core Preference Score. Signal 4 is deliberately kept out of the score (see below).

1
Years of experience In the score · 45%

The strongest signal. Patients default to experience.

2
Ratings In the score · 35%

Used to confirm or override experience.

3
Fees In the score · 20% (Fair Fees Index)

Not the cheapest, but reasonable for the doctor's experience and specialty.

4
Gender, in specific specialties Not scored · patient-controlled filter

Gynecology patients leaned toward female doctors; andrology patients toward male doctors.

The Fix: A Two-Layer Scoring Model

Preference and trust aren't the same kind of signal, so we split the model into two layers. Reliability has to gate visibility, not just adjust it.

Layer 1 · Core Preference Score
Years of Experience
45%
Patient Rating
35%
Fair Fees Index
20%
Layer 2 · Trust Gate

Attendance % and profile quality form one multiplier. Poor attendance or a weak profile pulls the whole score down. No amount of experience or good reviews makes up for it.

Core Preference Score × Trust Gate (0.6–1.0) = Final Score

Fair Fees Index: each fee is compared with the peer average for the same city tier, experience bracket and degree. At or below the benchmark scores near full marks. Well above it scores proportionally lower.

Profile Quality, Beyond Just Completeness

A complete profile isn't always a trustworthy one. Beyond checking filled fields, we bulk-cleaned photo backgrounds across the directory and scored photos on expression, blur and framing.

Decision

Gender: a deliberate exclusion. Gender was a real, measurable signal in some specialties. Ranking doctors by it would have been unfair to them. So it stayed out of the score and became a patient-controlled filter in those specialties: the preference served openly, not decided silently by an algorithm.
