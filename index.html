01 Lenskart@Home ● Live
₹1.8Cr
Personalised Home Trials
The Problem

80% of customers dropped off the Home Trial landing page at step one, before even selecting an address or slot. We called people who'd landed on the page but abandoned it that early to find out why.

“The store is already close to my house. If I go there, I'll get much more variety.”

Recurring verbatim from multiple discovery calls
The Real Insight

Customers weren't rejecting home trials. They were rejecting a kit they had no say over.

With no say over which frames showed up, booking felt like a gamble. A store visit felt certain. This wasn't a distance problem or a trust-in-service problem; it was a control problem.

30%
Of online returns trace back to fit or style issues; the exact uncertainty a home trial is supposed to remove. If customers didn't trust the kit, the trial couldn't do its job.
⚠
Constraint

No significant backend or inventory investment until demand was validated. Prove the hypothesis cheaply before committing engineering to a full integration.

The MVP: Testing Control, Not Variety

The hypothesis: it wasn't more frames customers needed, it was the act of choosing. So we tested that in isolation. Using an existing Lenskart product listing page, we let customers browse and "select" frames for their trial; from the same ~150 frames the agent was already carrying. Nothing changed in the backend. The agent showed up with the exact same kit as before, just now hand-picked by the customer on paper.

If bookings moved without a single new frame in the kit, we'd know: choice itself was the lever, not variety. It was the cheapest possible way to test that.

+7%
Increase in appointments booked in the pilot area; the control hypothesis held.

Product walkthrough; post-MVP live flow

The Real Launch: Real Inventory, Real Choice

With demand validated, we built the actual thing. Customers now browse real inventory from their nearby Lenskart store and select the specific frames they want brought home; not a curated kit standing in for choice, but their own list, live across 5 cities.

This also means better supply utilisation; the same store inventory now serves both walk-ins and home trials. But the incremental revenue itself comes from the lift in home trial bookings this unlocked.

“This is a great feature, we were too busy to go to the store so decided to do a home trial; you should make it a paid service, it is so premium.”

Customer who ended up purchasing 3 frames
⚡
Fun Fact

Nobody told agents to do this; but once they could see what customers had picked, they started calling them before appointments to discuss frames. We built a selection feature. Agents turned it into a styling concierge.

₹1.8Cr
Incremental annualised revenue across 5 cities, from the lift in bookings once customers could pick their own frames.
The Bigger Problem

Launching home trials was the easy half. Scaling them meant going beyond a dedicated agent workforce; every Lenskart store needed to become a home trial hub.

  • Gives stores a reason to reach beyond walk-in customers
  • Gives online customers a real, personal store connect
  • Adds home trials to what a store can offer, not just what an agent delivers

Making that work meant answering a harder question first: which store owns which customer?

Wanna see how we solved that?

02 Lenskart · Omni · ● Live
1 Customer, 1 Experience
Truly Omnichannel Experience for Customers
The Problem

When a Lenskart customer buys in-store, a staff member is right there to nudge them toward a repair or exchange if something's slightly off; fit, alignment, comfort. Online customers get none of that. The order lands at their door, something feels wrong, and with no one to talk them through it, they go straight to a return.

7%
Return rate for offline (in-store) orders
18%
Return rate for online orders; no one there to intervene

The fix wasn't just closing that gap. It was making every store accountable for a defined territory; every customer in it, online, offline, or home trial, becomes that store's responsibility.

Three Approaches Evaluated

All three sound reasonable. All three try to infer which store a customer has a relationship with. The data said that's the wrong question entirely.

Distance-based

Assign each order to the nearest store within 10km. Simple and deterministic.

✗ Failed  ;  Badly skewed in dense catchments; one store received ~600 orders in 4 months, several got zero
Last QMS (Eye Test) Store

Assign to the store where the customer's most recent eye test happened. Relationship-based.

✗ Failed  ;  Only 31% of QMS stores matched the customer's actual nearest store
Combination

QMS where available, distance everywhere else. Best of both worlds?

✗ Failed  ;  Two attribution classes, impossible to explain or set fair targets against
💬
Why "last visited" broke down

We spoke with customers who'd bought from a store far from home. The pattern: some had visited a store near their workplace, not home; others wanted more variety than their nearest store carried; a few simply didn't know a closer store existed. Last visit wasn't loyalty; it was coincidence.

The goal was never to find the store a customer prefers. It's to find the store best positioned to physically serve them.

The Solution: Polygon Attribution

Polygon attribution; inspired by Walmart's hex-grid catchment model, built from tiles of 0.1km²; answers exactly that: if this customer needs to walk into a store tomorrow, which one is realistically on their doorstep? No QMS preference, no relationship-inference. Deterministic and auditable; every delivery address belongs to exactly one store, no overlap, no dead zones. The polygon only changes when a new store opens.

The Dashboard: One Book of Business

We built a unified "Your Territory" view inside the store POS; every store sees its polygon, its online and walk-in orders together, and one set of targets across both. Ownership meant giving stores real tools to act on it:

  • 48-hour check-in calls; store agents call online customers 48 hours post-delivery to catch issues early and nudge a repair or exchange instead of a return
  • Proactive home trial pitches; stores can now reach online customers who dropped off mid-browse in their territory: "we saw you're interested in this frame; we have it in store and can send someone to your home to try it."

Live store POS; territory map + unified revenue view

💡
Customer Insight

We assumed customers would naturally gravitate to their nearest store. 25% didn't; they were buying from stores 5km+ away, on the way to work or mid-errand. Proximity doesn't predict preference; it just predicts who's responsible for you now.

41 → 60
Online customer NPS, since territory ownership and 48-hour check-ins went live.
03 Bajaj Finserv Health · Health Tech · Data ● Live
₹7L MRR
Smart Health Reports: Decoding Health Stats
The Dead Feature

Health Files let customers upload their lab reports to the platform. Almost nobody did. Rather than optimising the upload flow, we went back to customers to understand why.

⚡
Fun Fact

The few who did use Health Files were uploading everything from prescriptions to Aadhaar cards; Health Files had quietly become a general-purpose document locker nobody asked for.

“Why give my reports to a platform that does nothing with them?”

What customers actually told us
The Technical Challenge

Lab reports from different labs use different names, units, and formats for the same parameter. We had to standardise before we could explain anything.

📄 Raw PDF
Lab reports in different formats, layouts, units
🔍 OCR
Extract every parameter, value, and unit from the document
🔗 LOINC Mapping
Map each parameter to its universal medical ID; normalise across labs
🧠 Pareto Scope
Top 20% of AHC packages cover 80% of parameters; build those first.
🌟 Smart Report
Plain English explanations + historical trend for every parameter
The Product

Every parameter explained in plain English; what it means, where you stand, what's critical, how to improve it; plus a historical trend compiled across all past reports.

Outcome
  • A patient staring at a high HbA1c reading is the exact right time to recommend a diabetes consultation
  • ₹7L incremental MRR from health service recommendations surfaced inside the report
💡
Customer Insight

75% of customers googled their out-of-range parameters the moment they got their report. We built that into the report; and turned the anxiety into a recommendation.

04 Bajaj Finserv Health · ● Live
+18% CTR
Doctor Quality Score & Listing Reorder
The Problem

Doctor listings had no real ranking logic; sort order was effectively random. Doctors with poor attendance records ranked identically to doctors who consistently showed up on time, and doctors with incomplete profiles — missing degree, years of experience, description, or even a photo — could still surface in the first few scrolls, ahead of doctors with complete, trustworthy profiles.

What Patients Actually Weigh

Before rebuilding the ranking, we looked at what patients were actually weighing when choosing a doctor, and in what order.

1
Years of experience

The strongest signal; patients defaulted to more experienced doctors first.

2
Ratings

The second filter, used to validate or override the experience signal.

3
Fees

Not a hunt for the cheapest option, but a comparison against what felt reasonable for a doctor's experience and specialty.

4
Gender, in specific specialties

Patients booking a gynecologist skewed toward preferring a female doctor; patients booking an andrologist skewed toward preferring a male doctor.

The Fix: A Two-Layer Scoring Model

Rather than averaging every signal into one flat score, we split the model into two layers; preference signals and trust signals aren't the same kind of thing. A highly experienced doctor with a poor attendance record shouldn't be able to average their way to a high ranking — reliability has to gate visibility, not just adjust it.

Layer 2 · Trust Gate

Attendance % and Profile Quality combine into a single multiplier. A doctor with poor attendance or an incomplete, unprofessional profile has their entire score pulled down proportionally — no amount of experience or strong reviews can fully compensate for unreliability.

Core Preference Score × Trust Gate (0.6–1.0) = Final Score

Fair Fees Index, defined: each doctor's fee is benchmarked against the peer average for doctors in the same city tier, years-of-experience bracket, and degree. A doctor charging near or below that peer benchmark scores near full marks; one charging well above it receives a proportionally lower score.

Profile Quality, Beyond Just Completeness

A complete profile isn't necessarily a trustworthy one; a doctor could fill every field with a blurry, badly cropped, unsmiling photo. So beyond checking whether fields were filled in, we bulk-cleaned photo backgrounds across the entire doctor directory for visual consistency, and scored photo professionalism directly on expression, blur, and framing (full face captured vs. zoomed-out or cropped).

⚖
Gender: A Deliberate Exclusion

Gender was a real, measurable signal in patient behaviour for specific specialties — but we chose not to fold it into the algorithmic score. Ranking doctors by a sensitive attribute would have been unfair to doctors themselves. Instead, we surfaced it as a contextual, patient-controlled filter in specialties where the pattern was strong, so the platform could serve the preference transparently without an algorithm making that call silently.
